## 1. Summary

Six questionnaire responses were analysed to understand how students find learning help and whether a skill-exchange platform could fit their needs. Search by skill was selected by five respondents; offered/wanted skill profiles and the combined proficiency/language filter option were each selected by three.

Five respondents expressed positive interest in using the platform, but only two selected a willingness to receive help and share skills in return. Two wanted to receive help only, and two did not want to participate yet. Interest in the idea therefore cannot be treated as a commitment to reciprocal exchange.

The results support testing skill discovery and clear profiles. They also identify questions about competence, practical arrangements, and teaching readiness that the first prototype should address. This exploratory sample does not establish university-wide demand, successful reciprocal matching, or learning improvement.

## 2. Method

The study used an online questionnaire in Russian. The analysis includes all six response records, including neutral answers and internally inconsistent selections. The questionnaire replaces the discussion format in the original SSE-2 brief; the repository filename follows the task's required deliverable path.

The source is the six-row response extract, checked against three spreadsheet screenshots. P1-P6 identify the source rows, not chronological order. Dates follow the project owner's correction and supersede the dates displayed in the screenshots. Exact submission times are not used.

Four respondents reported bachelor's study and two reported master's study. Recruitment channel, university affiliation, field of study, response rate, and individual consent selections are not available in the analysis extract. Names and contact details are excluded. The sample should not be presented as representative of Astana IT University or the wider student population.

The notes translate and paraphrase selected answer options. They are not interview quotations. Detailed learning incidents and follow-up explanations were not collected, and the optional comments contained no substantive text.

### Measures and counting rules

Response labels D01-D14 are local evidence identifiers. For example, P3-D05 refers to P3's main reported difficulty.

| Label | Measure |
| --- | --- |
| D01 | Self-reported study level |
| D02 | Frequency of needing another person's help with learning in the preceding three months |
| D03 | First method used in the most recent help search during the same three months |
| D04 | Outcome of that search |
| D05 | Main difficulty |
| D06 | Areas in which help is wanted |
| D07 | Possible skills to share |
| D08 | Participation preference |
| D09 | Interaction format |
| D10 | Weekly time budget |
| D11 | Features selected for a first version |
| D12 | Information that would increase comfort with a partner |
| D13 | Hypothetical intention to use the platform |
| D14 | Additional comment |

Two distinctions matter for interpreting the history questions. D02 asks how often help was **needed**, not how many searches or sessions took place. D03 asks which method was used **first**, not every channel tried. Both questions refer to the preceding three months relative to the response date.

Counts are the number of respondents selecting an option, with a denominator of six. D06, D07, and D11 permit multiple selections, so their totals can exceed six. No order of preference is inferred within a respondent's selections. The combined proficiency/language option does not measure the importance of those filters separately.

## 5. Evidence-based findings

**F01 - Skill-based search was the most selected feature.** Five selected search by skill (P1/P2/P3/P5/P6-D11). P1 and P5 selected difficulty knowing where to find a suitable person (D05), although P5's history is inconsistent. This supports testing discovery, not a claim that all students have the same problem.

**F02 - Competence and comfort need attention.** P2 and P3 found it difficult to assess knowledge (D05), despite reporting suitable help. Comfort signals varied: reviews for P1 and P3, student status for P2, work samples for P5, and a personal recommendation for P6 (D12). No trust mechanism was tested.

**F03 - Finding someone did not always resolve the learning need.** P1 found someone but could not agree on a session; P6 received help that did not resolve the question (D04). The responses do not establish whether timing, expectations, teaching ability, or another factor caused these outcomes.

**F04 - Interest and reciprocal participation are different.** Five expressed positive use intention (D13), but only P2 and P3 chose to both receive and provide help (D08). P5's definite interest coexists with no current participation or planned time. Skills listed in D07 must not be interpreted as teaching commitments.

**F05 - Format and time preferences vary.** The responses include flexible, in-person-only, and online-only preferences, together with several time-budget categories (P1-P6-D09/D10). These are stated preferences, including those of respondents not ready to participate; actual shared availability remains unknown.

**F06 - Profiles and filters are candidates for testing.** Offered/wanted profiles were selected by P1, P3, and P5; the combined level/language filter option by P1, P3, and P6 (D11). This supports evaluating those designs. It does not establish that each filter is essential or that the profile produces a suitable reciprocal match.

## 7. Implications for the platform

The proposed decisions below connect the response evidence with the project direction. R01-R08 are local labels, not new YouTrack issues. Implementation priorities must be reconciled with SSE-6.

| Proposed decision | Evidence | Action and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R01 - Prioritise skill-based search | F01; P1/P2/P3/P5/P6-D11 | Test whether students can find relevant help by a concrete topic. Measure relevance and ease of discovery, not just search-result volume. |
| R02 - Keep offered and wanted skills separate | F04, F06; P1/P3/P5-D11; D06/D07 | Use clear skill descriptions and explicit teaching readiness. Do not automatically publish every listed capability as an active teaching offer. |
| R03 - Test transparent direct reciprocal suggestions | F04; A03; SSE-3 | Retain simple two-way matching and short explanations as the project approach. The survey does not validate the algorithm. Confirm topic compatibility and mutual interest in a pilot. |
| R04 - Make requests and agreement explicit | F03, F04; P1-D04; P5-D11; SSE-3 | Retain request, accept, and decline actions. State what each person offers and wants before acceptance. Check cancellation, completion, and rescheduling rules against SSE-6. |
| R05 - Validate filters and session arrangements | F05, F06; P1/P3/P6-D11; P2-D11 | Test language and level separately. Support clear agreement on time and format within the agreed scope. Availability matching and calendar integration require further justification. |
| R06 - Test useful partner information before public ratings | F02; P2/P3-D05; D12 | Evaluate concise experience descriptions and work samples. Keep student status distinct from competence. Treat reviews and reputation as future candidates under SSE-3. |
| R07 - Separate browsing from agreement to teach | F04; P1/P6-D07/D08; P5-D07/D08/D10/D13 | Make participation voluntary and clarify what happens when no reciprocal match exists. Whether one-way help is supported remains a product-scope decision. |
| R08 - Review extra communication features against scope | P2-D11; P6-D11 | Chat and notifications were each selected once. Compare them with simpler arrangements and the SSE-6 scope rather than adding them automatically. |

### Consistency with the literature review

The team's SSE-3 literature review, sections 6 and 7, provides the design baseline: offered/wanted profiles, search, direct reciprocal matches with brief explanations, and voluntary exchange requests. This survey adds local preference evidence and exposes the gap between interest and willingness to reciprocate.

Availability, language, proficiency, and format filters still need focused validation. Public ratings and machine-learning or graph-based ranking remain future candidates; formal grading remains outside the current scope. The questionnaire did not evaluate those mechanisms. These scope decisions follow SSE-3 and are distinct from the survey findings.

## 8. Limitations and next validation

The study is exploratory. Six responses with an undocumented recruitment process cannot establish population-level demand. The answers are self-reports based largely on closed options, with no detailed incident narratives or follow-up explanations. Broad categories do not establish proficiency, teaching readiness, or a viable match.

P5's history remains inconsistent and is preserved with that qualification. The combined filter option does not separate language from proficiency. Hypothetical use intention and stated time budgets are not evidence of actual participation. Recruitment details, university affiliations, consent selections, and the form URL were not included in the analysis record.

The next checks should be practical:

1. Clarify P5's history and teaching readiness if follow-up is possible, keeping the original response and clarification as separate records.
2. Collect concrete learning examples to understand P1's failed agreement and P6's unresolved help, rather than assuming their causes.
3. Test skill search and profiles with realistic topics. Check whether two willing students can identify a useful reciprocal match and agree on time and format.
4. Record accepted and completed exchanges, usefulness to each participant, unmatched cases, and willingness to return.

## 9. Conclusion

The responses support a focused prototype for finding learning help and understanding what a potential partner can offer. They also show that reciprocal exchange needs direct validation: five expressed positive intention to use the platform, while only two selected willingness to help in return. The project should test the complete exchange journey before claiming a working reciprocal community or a learning benefit.
